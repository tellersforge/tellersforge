# 1. Before You Begin

*Set: Core*

This is the Core rulebook of Teller's Forge. It is the second of three layers. The first is **Teller's Forge Basic**, a short booklet that teaches the whole game in an evening with six species, six professions, eighteen skills and twelve powers. This book, **Teller's Forge Core**, adds to Basic: the full way of checking a skill, more ways to fight, more ways to grow, and the doors into the Core lexicons, which hold the hundreds of skills, powers, weapons, items, creatures and species the Core game plays with. The third layer, the **Expansions**, adds the rare and the strange.

> ### The one promise of the layers
>
> Nothing you learned in Basic is overturned here. Core adds; it never changes. Every Basic rule is repeated in this book word for word where it belongs, with the Core additions after it. A Basic character can sit at a Core table, and a Basic reader can pick this book up and lose nothing.

If you have never played a roleplaying game, start with the Basic booklet, or read the next three sections here. If you already know roleplaying games, skim them and read from *A tour of your character*.

### What a roleplaying game is

A roleplaying game (an RPG) is a game of telling a story together. It is played around a table, or online, by a small group of friends, usually three to six, with pencils, paper and dice. There is no board and there are no pieces to move. The game happens in conversation.

- **The players.** Each player invents one person, their character, and decides everything that character says and does: "I climb the wall," "I ask the innkeeper about the missing girl," "I draw my sword."
- **The Game Master.** One person does not play a character. The Game Master (the GM) describes the world, plays everyone else in it (the innkeeper, the bandits, the queen, the dragon), and says what happens because of what the characters do.
- **The dice.** Most of the time the conversation is enough. When the outcome is uncertain, the dice decide. Your character's numbers, written on a character sheet, say how likely they are to succeed.
- **Nobody wins.** There is no score to beat and no end to reach first. The players work together; the Game Master is not their enemy. Characters can fail, get hurt, even die, and those moments are often the ones people remember.
- **Sessions and campaigns.** A session is one evening's play, typically two to four hours. A story that runs over many sessions, with the same characters, is a campaign. Characters grow between sessions.

**What it sounds like.**  
Game Master: The rope bridge sways over the gorge. Halfway across, one plank is missing. The wind is picking up.  
Player 1: Mira goes first. She tests each plank with her foot before she trusts it.  
Game Master: Careful enough that she won't fall by accident, but the gap needs a jump. Roll for it.  
Player 1 (rolls): Made it!  
Game Master: Mira lands on the far side. Behind you, Tomas, you see Mira make it.  
Player 2: Tomas is afraid of heights… (Tomas has the flaw Fear; the Game Master asks for a flaw roll to see whether his fear takes over.)

### What Teller's Forge is

Teller's Forge is a skill-based, roll-under roleplaying game for every era, from the stone age to the far future. Its main ideas:

- **You play a person, not a pile of numbers built to win.** Your character will be good at some things, bad at others, and now and then their flaws will get the better of them. That is the point.
- **Any world, any age.** Stone-age hunters, a legion, knights and wizards, modern soldiers, starship crews. The same rules cover them all. Magic, psychic powers and strange technology are an extra layer: if your world has none, you skip that chapter.
- **Roll under your own numbers.** Your sheet shows how good you are. To succeed, you roll at or under that number on a twenty-sided die. High numbers on your sheet are always good.
- **One die per attribute.** Most skills depend on more than one quality. A blacksmith needs strength, a steady hand and stamina. In Core, a skill check rolls one twenty-sided die for each attribute the skill uses, and every die has to succeed. Basic rolled one die and took 4 off the number instead; both ways are in this book.
- **Life-story character creation.** You build a character the way a life happens: what they were born as, when and where they grew up, their family's place in the world, and what they became.
- **Flaws are part of the fun.** Every character has flaws that can take over at the worst moment. Playing them well earns rewards.
- **Few numbers in play.** The arithmetic is done once, when you make the character, and written on the sheet. During play you mostly just roll and compare.
- **Your table decides.** Many choices, such as how quickly characters heal or how deadly a lucky hit is, are table settings your group picks together before play (chapter 14).

### Reading dice

Dice are named by how many sides they have: a d20 has twenty sides (1 to 20), a d6 six, a d4 four. A number in front says how many to roll and add together; a number after a plus sign is added to the total.

- **1d20:** roll one twenty-sided die.
- **2d6+4:** roll two six-sided dice, add them, then add 4. The result is between 6 and 16.
- **1d4+3:** roll one four-sided die and add 3. The result is between 4 and 7.
- A **natural 1** or **natural 20** means the die itself showed 1 or 20, before anything was added.

> ### Two editions, one game
>
> This is the **Teller's Forge 20** edition: every roll is a d20. The **Teller's Forge 100** edition is the same book with every rolled-against number and every modifier multiplied by five, rolled on percentile dice, so the odds come out the same to the point. A natural 1 is 01 to 05 there; a natural 20 is 96 to 00. Nothing else differs.

### The words this book uses

| Word                 | What it means                                                                                                                                                                                     |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Attribute            | One of the ten basic numbers that describe your character: Strength, Dexterity and the rest. Rolled when the character is made.                                                                   |
| Value                | The finished number for a skill, a power or a combat move. It is what you roll against.                                                                                                           |
| Modifier             | Any number added to or taken from a value, once: a +3 from your profession, a −3 for a hard task.                                                                                                 |
| Lvl/Mod              | The sheet column for a skill's level or a power's level. A skill's level is also the modifier for that skill; a power's level is also the modifier for that power. The column carries both names. |
| Rerolls              | The second way a skill grows once its Lvl/Mod is full: chances to roll a failed die again. Core only.                                                                                             |
| Check                | A dice roll to see whether your character succeeds at something hard.                                                                                                                             |
| Fate die             | In a Core check, the odd-coloured die rolled for the skill's bold attribute. A natural 1 on it succeeds, a natural 20 fails, whatever the others say.                                             |
| Rule / Rule of thumb | A is a fixed mechanic, played the same way by everyone. A is strong advice; the Game Master makes the final call.                                                                                 |

### A tour of your character

Here is every part of a Core character. For each: what it is, what it does when you play, and where its rules are. Making the character, step by step, is in the **Characters / Core** book; chapter 3 of this book says how the two books fit together.

### Species

**What it is:** what your character was born as. Basic offered six: baseline human, dwarf, high elf, gnome-kind, orc and lizardfolk. Core offers fifty-four, grouped in families, each with its own entry in Characters / Core; the Expansions add hundreds more. **What it does in play:** it changes your attributes, gives the talents of your body (keen noses, night vision, wings, claws), sets your size (which changes how hard you are to hit, how much harm you can take, and whether you fit through a door) and sometimes gives powers. **Where:** Characters / Core.

### Era

**What it is:** the level of technology your character grew up with, a point on a line from stone tools to the far future. Basic had no era: a Basic character is assumed to know the ordinary things of whatever setting the table plays in. Core makes that concrete. **What it does in play:** anything invented after your era is unknown to you: a Roman has never seen a computer, and every skill that needs one is at −6, whatever else the character learned. Gear works the same way. **Where:** Characters / Core.

### Culture and social standing

**What they are:** where your character grew up (forest hunters, steppe riders, a feudal kingdom, a modern city, an orbital habitat) and your family's place in it (outcast, bonded, labourer, commoner, craft family, merchant family, gentry, high nobility). Basic left both out. **What they do in play:** they give the skills everyone raised that way picks up, the weapon your people traditionally fight with, and the things your upbringing neglected; social standing also sets how much you own at the start. **Where:** Characters / Core.

### Profession

**What it is:** what your character became. Basic's six (Fighter, Scout, Thief, Healer, Mage, Engineer) stand unchanged in Core, and Core breaks them out: a Fighter may be a Soldier, a Guard, a Duellist, a Pit fighter, a Space trooper, an Officer; a Scout may be a Ranger; and so on through forty professions. **What it does in play:** it gives your strongest skills (Lvl/Mod +3), two supporting skills (+1), two neglected skills (−1), your extra Life and your starting kit. It comes last in the life story, so it has the final word. **Where:** Characters / Core.

### Attributes

**What they are:** ten numbers for your character's basic qualities, usually between 6 and 16. Higher is always better.

| Attribute    | Short | What it covers                                                 |
|--------------|-------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Strength     | STR   | muscle, lifting, hitting hard                                  |
| Dexterity    | DEX   | fine motor control: hands, aim, delicate work                  |
| Agility      | AGI   | the whole body: balance, climbing, dodging, riding             |
| Endurance    | END   | stamina, toughness, health; resisting poison, disease and cold |
| Intelligence | INT   | learning, reasoning, memory                                    |
| Wisdom       | WIS   | judgement, instinct, willpower                                 |
| Perception   | PER   | the senses: noticing, searching, listening                     |
| Charisma     | CHA   | presence, charm, leadership                                    |
| Courage      | COU   | nerve, facing fear; also who acts first                        |
| Luck         | LUCK  | fortune; rolled when chance alone decides (chapter 2)          |

**What they do in play:** almost everything. Every skill check rolls against attributes. Your Attack in combat comes from attributes. Endurance sets how much harm you can take. Courage decides who acts first and resists fear. An attribute can be rolled on its own for raw effort: forcing a stuck door is a Strength roll.

> ### Playing with other games' attributes
>
> Teller's Forge is built to carry any story and any system. Where another game has one attribute that Teller's Forge splits (its "dexterity" may be Teller's Forge's Dexterity and Agility together), use whichever Teller's Forge attribute fits the action. Where another game has an attribute Teller's Forge lacks, it is usually two of ours rolled together: intuition is Wisdom and Perception, one die each. Luck is here so that games built on luck can be played as written; a table that does not want it leaves the Luck line blank and uses the Game Master's judgement instead.

### Flaws

**What they are:** nine weaknesses, one tied to each attribute except Luck, each with a score usually between 2 and 9. In Basic every character has the same nine standard flaws. In Core you may keep them or swap any of them for a flaw of your own choosing from the catalogue of over a hundred and fifty: fears, vices, bad habits, and the drawbacks of a hard world. **What they do in play:** when a situation touches a flaw, you roll a d20; at or under the score, the flaw takes over, and you play it out. Light, funny flaws have high scores and happen often; grave ones have low scores and happen rarely. Playing a flaw well earns experience. Over time, flaws shrink. **Where:** chapter 5; Characters / Core for the catalogue.

### Skills

**What they are:** everything a person can learn to do. Basic's eighteen broad skills (Athletics, Stealth, Persuasion and the rest) stand unchanged in Core, and Core splits each into its parts: Athletics becomes Climbing, Jumping, Swimming and Running; Survival becomes Tracking, Foraging, Fire-Making and Wayfinding; and so on, fifty-five Core skills in all, each with its own entry in Skills / Core. Each skill lists the attributes it uses, the first in bold. **What they do in play:** when your character tries something uncertain, you make a check with the matching skill (chapter 2). Every skill has a level: 0 is untrained; +1 to +5 is the Lvl/Mod added to every die; beyond +5, rerolls. **Where:** chapter 2; Skills / Core.

### Combat values and weapon groups

**What they are:** fighting is built into every character; it is not a skill. From your attributes you work out, once, the five combat factors of every Teller's Forge game, Attack, Parry, Dodge, Armour and Range, with Damage, Life and Initiative as their results. Basic worked them out from one generic weapon group, "the weapons of your setting". Core adds weapon groups (Swords, Bows, Spears, Brawling, Handguns, Energy Weapons and so on), each with its own three attributes and its own level, so a swordsman and an archer are different fighters. **Where:** chapters 6 to 8; Gear / Core.

### Powers

**What they are:** abilities beyond ordinary people: magic, psychic gifts, miracles, mutant talents, super-science. Basic's twelve Mage powers stand unchanged in Core; Core adds sixty-three, and higher versions of many (Fire Blast II, III), in Powers / Core. **What they do in play:** a power is checked exactly like a skill and grows exactly like one. Casting spends Energy from your Magic Energy Pool. **Where:** chapter 12; Powers / Core.

### Gear

**What it is:** everything your character carries. Every item has a weight, an era and a price. **What it does in play:** weapons set your damage, armour your protection, tools make skills possible, and weight limits how much you can carry before you tire. **Where:** chapters 8 and 11; Gear / Core.

### Experience and growth

At the end of each session the Game Master hands out experience points (XP). Enough XP gives a Level Up: a chance to raise an attribute, a chance to shrink a flaw, and two points for skills or powers. Skills you practise hard can also improve on their own. Characters start at level 1 with 0 XP. **Where:** chapter 4.

### Fatigue and healing

Hard effort wears a character down in stages, and lost Life comes back with rest. How fast is a table setting. **Where:** chapters 9 and 10.

### How this book is organised

- **Chapters 1 to 3** get you started: this introduction, how checks work, and where your character is made.
- **Chapters 4 and 5** cover growth and flaws.
- **Chapters 6 to 11** cover fighting, armour, fatigue, healing and gear.
- **Chapters 12 and 13** cover powers and long projects; skip them if your world has no powers.
- **Chapter 14** is the Table Settings your group decides together; **chapter 15** is the cheat sheets; **chapter 16** says where the tables of skills, powers, gear, species and creatures are.

> ### The most important rule
>
> The table having fun matters more than anything written here. If your group wants to change a rule, change it. Whenever a rule in this book sounds strict, remember this box.

### First step, deeper steps

Every rule starts with a first step. If the first step answers the question for everyone at the table, you are done. Deeper steps are there only for groups that want more detail. Nothing is lost by stopping at the first step. Basic is the first step of everything.

### Roleplay first

Dice are there to add surprise, not to replace the story. When everyone at the table already knows what a character would do, and how well they would do it, let it happen. Roll when it adds something: when the outcome is uncertain, when the odds are impossible, or when a sure thing going wrong would make the table laugh. A whole campaign can be played without a single skill check, and that is still Teller's Forge.

### What you need

- Several twenty-sided dice (d20), one of them a different colour: the fate die.
- A few six-sided dice (d6) and a four-sided die (d4), for damage and for making characters.
- A character sheet for each player, a pencil and an eraser.
- The Table Settings page (chapter 14), filled in together at the first meeting.
- The Core lexicon books your game uses: Skills, Powers, Gear, Characters, Bestiary. A fantasy table with no technology never opens half of Gear; a modern table never opens Powers.
